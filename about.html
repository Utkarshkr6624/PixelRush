ABOUT PIXEL RUSH

PixelRush is a free browser arcade built by one person in the margins of a day job. It holds 27 instant-play games, it runs on a stack of plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, and it will never ask you for an account, a card or a download. This page explains who is behind it, what it believes, and how the thing actually works under the neon.

Who is behind it

One developer, one garage, no publisher

PixelRush is maintained by a single developer, Utkarsh, who builds it in the evenings and at weekends alongside a day job. There is no studio, no art department and no publishing deal. The cabinets, the artwork, the sound and the words on this site were all made in the same handful of files, in the same editor, by the same person.

That is worth saying plainly, because it explains almost everything else you will read here. There is no growth target, no conversion funnel and nobody whose job it is to make you play longer. When a cabinet is finished, it goes live — and if one ever is not finished, it says so on the card rather than pretending. When a page is too slow on a mid-range phone, it gets smaller.

What the project is not

  • Not a company. There is no legal entity behind the site, no support desk and no service level agreement. The full terms of use spell out what that means in practice.
  • Not a paywall. No game here is locked, timed or trimmed. There is no trial clock, no "remove ads" button, no premium tier and nothing to buy. Advertising pays for the hosting; it never touches the games.
  • Not a content farm. The pages exist to explain the games, not to rank for a keyword. The privacy policy is short because we hold almost nothing ourselves — see the advertising section there for what the ad providers do see.
  • Not a storefront for anyone else's games. Every title here was written for this site.

If something on the site is broken, wrong or quietly embarrassing, the fastest way to get it fixed is an email to Utkarsh6624@gmail.com. Real bugs get fixed in the next sitting. Feature requests get argued about honestly, which is often more useful than a quiet no.

The philosophy

Free forever, because the maths is trivial

A static site costs almost nothing to host, and a canvas game is a few kilobytes of JavaScript with no server round-trip at all. There is no cost centre here to pass on, so there is nothing to charge you for. The price is zero, permanently, and that is not a trial period — it is the whole arrangement.

No signup, because there is nothing to sign in to

An account would mean an email address, a password, a server, a database and a privacy policy about both. Instead, your scores and preferences live in your own browser's local storage, on your own device. Nothing is uploaded, so there is nothing for us to lose, leak or hand over. Clearing your browser data clears your high scores, and that is a trade this site makes on purpose.

No account, no analytics — but yes, ads

Two different things get lumped together as "tracking", so here they are apart. We run no analytics script of our own and we build no profile of you: there is no server holding your scores, your visits or your email address, because there is no server. Your high scores and preferences never leave your browser.

The site does carry advertising, and that is a different arrangement. Ads are served by third parties, and that normally means they can set cookies or similar storage, see your IP address and build a profile from it. We do not control that, we do not get handed the data, and we have not chosen a provider yet, so no name is listed in the privacy policy — it describes the category instead. The games stay exactly as they were: free, whole, no timer, nothing unlocked by paying.

No download, because a download is a tax

A download means a decision, and a decision is where most people leave. Here a game is a web page: one URL, one tab, playable within a second of clicking. It works on a locked-down library machine, on a school Chromebook and on a phone with half a bar of signal, because the same files serve all three.

Short sessions, on purpose

Most titles here last between one and eight minutes and restarts take a single key. They are built for the gap between two meetings, the queue at the shop, the five minutes before a train arrives. A game you cannot start in one click, or finish before your stop, is the wrong game for this site — so the whole catalogue is designed around short runs and instant retries.

Under the neon

How the arcade is actually built

No framework, no bundler, no build step. If you clone the repository and open index.html in a text editor, what you read is what the browser gets. There is no compiler to install, no dependency tree to audit and no toolchain that can break on a new release. The whole site is HTML documents, three stylesheets and two scripts.

Static files, served as-is

Every page is a plain .html file with its own title, description and canonical URL, so each one can be linked, bookmarked and indexed on its own. There is no server rendering, no client-side router and no single-page-app shell. A page is either served or it is not, and it is readable with JavaScript switched off.

Canvas games with a shared player shell

The games themselves are drawn on an HTML canvas and driven by a requestAnimationFrame loop with a fixed logical resolution, so they scale cleanly from a 360 px phone to a large monitor without blurring. The page they live on, play.html, is a shared shell: it reads the game name from the address, loads that one game file, and adds the frame chrome, the pause behaviour and the score readout. New games plug into the shell rather than shipping their own page.

Scores live in localStorage

Personal bests, the games you have played and your motion preference are stored under namespaced keys in your browser's local storage. Nothing is sent anywhere. The upside is instant load and zero latency; the trade-off is that scores follow the device, not the person, and a private window or a cleared cache wipes them. That trade is the price of having no account and no server.

The depth and parallax layer system

Each full-bleed section is a .scene containing stacked .layer elements, numbered depth-0 to depth-5. On scroll, one transform per layer is written from a single value, so the grid horizon, the glow blobs, the floating shards, the copy plane and the foreground particles move at different rates over one shared number. It is a fake third dimension made of about forty lines of JavaScript, and it costs no images: the artwork is CSS gradients and inline SVG.

The layers that carry text are marked layer--flow. That single class opts them out of the bleed inset the decorative layers need, and it is the reason the prose on this page is positioned normally instead of being dragged off-screen.

Accessibility

What we promise, and how it is enforced

A neon arcade is exactly the kind of site that gets built with motion everywhere, low contrast and zero keyboard support. These are the commitments that stop that happening, and the mechanism behind each one.

Reduced motion is respected, and testable

Every decorative animation — parallax, drifting shards, floating particles, the scrolling ticker — is switched off when the operating system asks for reduced motion. Reveal animations resolve to their final state immediately, so nothing is ever left invisible. There is also a motion toggle in the header, so you can switch the whole thing off yourself; your choice is remembered on this device.

Keyboard first, on every cabinet

Every game is playable with a keyboard alone: arrows or WASD to move, space to act, R to restart, P to pause. No action is mouse-only, and focus order follows the visual order, so tabbing through a page reads it top to bottom. Focus rings are visible by default and are never removed in favour of a custom effect.

Screen readers get the real content

Decorative layers are hidden from assistive technology, so a screen reader hears headings and paragraphs rather than a pile of empty divs. Each page has one h1, heading levels never skip, every link describes its destination out loud — "Play Neon Snake", not "click here" — and every icon-only control carries a label. A skip link is the first thing in the document, so the navigation is one keypress away.

Contrast that survives the glow

Neon on near-black looks better at low contrast, so the type scale is tuned until body copy and the dimmer labels clear the contrast thresholds rather than stopping at what looks moody. Colour is never the only signal: genre, difficulty and run length are all written out in text, and the state of a cabinet is always written out, never signalled by colour alone.

Mobile-first and honest about it

The layout is built for a 360 px phone first and widened from there, so nothing scrolls sideways on a small screen. Touch targets are large enough for a thumb in a moving queue, pointer actions never depend on hover, and the games accept touch, mouse and keyboard without a mode switch.

How it got here

Four years, one file at a time

No launch date, no marketing push. The site grew the way a collection does: one cabinet finished, published, then the next one.

  1. 01

    One HTML file and a grid

    The first cabinet was a single hand-written file with a moving square on a grid. It was playable on a laptop, unplayable on a phone, and it proved the whole idea: if a game is one file, the hosting is free and the page is fast.

    2024 · the first prototype
  2. 02

    Neon, layers and the depth system

    The visual language came next: a synth horizon, glow blobs, floating shards and six parallax depth planes driven from a single scroll value. It looked like an arcade rather than a spreadsheet, and it cost nothing to serve because none of it is an image.

    Late 2024 · the look
  3. 03

    A shared player shell and a catalogue

    Rebuilding the same pause button, score readout and restart key for every game was the obvious waste, so all games moved into one shell and the library became a grid you could filter. The catalogue passed a dozen games and started behaving like an arcade floor.

    2025 · the arcade
  4. 04

    Accessibility and the small print

    Motion toggles, real focus rings, honest link text, a skip link and a privacy policy written in plain English. The site got quieter, faster and smaller, and it did not lose any of its character doing it.

    2026 · today

The roadmap

Every cabinet is live — and every one is phone-playable

all twenty-seven cabinets are built, tested and clickable. Each one was played on a 390px touch screen as well as a desktop keyboard, so nothing here needs a mouse.

Live and playable — all twenty-seven

Every game in the library is finished. Open it and play without an account. A few to start on:

  • Neon Reaction — a reflex drill that measures the gap between seeing the signal and pressing the key.
  • Neon Snake — grow a light-streak through the grid until you box yourself in.
  • Blockfall — stack falling blocks and clear four lines before the grid fills.
  • Penalty Kick — five kicks against a keeper who reads your body line, so the corner is safe only if you commit to it.
  • Rogue Shift — a small turn-based roguelike where the map reshuffles and you descend exactly once.
  • The full library — 27 cabinets across arcade, puzzle, racing, strategy, casual and sports, each with its own length and difficulty.

What is genuinely still to come

The cabinet shelf is full. These are the things the site still does not do, listed so you are not left guessing:

  • Shared high-score boards — still being argued about, because they need a server, and a server is a cost and a privacy question.
  • More genres — the six aisles are covered, but each of them has room for two or three more cabinets.
  • Sound — every game is deliberately silent so it works anywhere without a mute button. A good synth soundtrack is a real project of its own.
  • Daily challenges — possible without a server if the seed is derived from the date, and that is the obvious next idea.

If a cabinet is a bit thin, gets in the way on your phone, or you simply want a twenty-fifth, email Utkarsh6624@gmail.com and say so — that is genuinely the most useful thing you can send.

The honest bit

A hobby project, not a business

PixelRush is not a company, not a startup and not a product with a roadmap that ends in an acquisition. It is one person's spare hours. It has no investors, no employees, no support contract and no guarantee that any individual cabinet will be updated on any particular date. The games page is the library, and the library is the product; if a suggestion does not fit the short-session idea, the honest answer will probably be no.

You should also know what the site deliberately does not do. There is no multiplayer, no cloud saves, no in-app purchases, no paywall and no premium tier. High scores are per-device, so switching phones leaves your best behind. Games are meant to be finished, forgotten and replayed, not accumulated. What the site does carry is advertising, which pays for the hosting and is served by other people — you can read exactly what that means in the privacy policy.

The site is maintained as it stands, with the same promise as on day one: free, no account, no download, on any screen. © 2026 PixelRush. The privacy policy and the terms of use say the same thing in the legal register.
